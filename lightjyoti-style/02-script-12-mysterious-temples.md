## #12: Kal Bhairav Temple, Ujjain (Madhya Pradesh) (0:35–1:30)

**VO:**
"Our journey begins in Ujjain, the city of Mahakal. Here stands the temple of Kal Bhairav, the fierce guardian form of Shiva.
And the offering here? **Not sweets. Not flowers. Liquor.**
The priest holds a saucer of liquor to the deity's lips, and devotees swear they watch it **empty before their eyes.** Every day, for as long as anyone can remember.
Where does it go? Some say the idol absorbs it. Sceptics have looked for hidden channels and cavities, and nobody has publicly shown one.
Belief or trick? Science or devotion? Ujjain doesn't answer. It just pours another cup.
But if a drinking deity surprised you, **the next temple defies gravity itself.**"

**Visuals:** night aarti in Ujjain, a saucer tilting toward a dark idol with vermilion on its face, a close-up on the liquid level dropping, a question mark dissolving into incense smoke.

## #11: Lepakshi Veerabhadra Temple, Andhra Pradesh (1:30–2:35)

**VO:**
"In Lepakshi, a 16th-century temple of the Vijayanagara era stands on about 70 carved stone pillars.
But **one of them doesn't rest fully on the ground.**
Visitors slide a cloth, a paper, even a thin stick **right under it**, from one side to the other.
The story goes that during British rule an engineer tried to find out how the roof stayed up. He shifted the pillar, and parts of the ceiling moved. He stopped immediately.
So is it floating? No. Engineers say the pillar has **slipped slightly out of line**, and the weight of the roof is carried by the pillars around it. It's an ancient structure balancing itself.
That's still remarkable: a 500-year-old roof that **survived its own mistake.**
And now we go where the sea itself becomes the priest…"

**Visuals:** a row of carved pillars, low-angle light through the gap, a cloth passing under the pillar, a sepia "British engineer" reconstruction, a simple animated diagram of the load shifting to neighbouring pillars.

## #10: Stambheshwar Mahadev, Kavi Kamboi (Gujarat) (2:35–3:35)

**VO:**
"On the coast of Gujarat, where the Mahi river meets the Gulf of Khambhat, there's a Shiva temple you can only visit **by appointment with the tide.**
Twice a day the sea rises and the entire shrine **disappears underwater.** Then the waters pull back, and the Shivling appears again as if it had just been reborn.
Locals call it the ocean's own abhishek.
Science has a simple answer: **tides.** The Gulf of Khambhat has some of the highest tidal ranges in India.
But standing there as the waves close over a temple and the bells go quiet… logic feels very small.
Temple timings here are printed on a **tide chart.** Where else in the world does that happen?"

**Visuals:** a time-lapse of the sea swallowing the shrine, devotees waiting on the shore, a printed tide timetable at the gate, a golden reveal as the water recedes.

## #9: Karni Mata Temple, Deshnoke (Rajasthan) (3:35–4:40)

**VO:**
"Remember the rats from the beginning? Welcome to Deshnoke, near Bikaner.
In this temple live **around 25,000 rats.** They're fed, protected and worshipped. Here they're called *kabbas*, 'little children'.
The belief: they are the reincarnated family of Karni Mata, a warrior-sage of the 14th–15th century. Food they've nibbled is taken as prasad.
And somewhere among the thousands of black rats there are a few **white ones.** Spot one, and you're said to be truly blessed.
Here's what makes scientists pause: this many rats, and no plague outbreak has ever been traced back to this temple. Researchers point to regular cleaning and the rats' contained environment, but the numbers still surprise everyone who visits.
If you think this is strange, wait until you see a temple **carved out of a mountain… from the top down.**"

**Visuals:** a marble courtyard alive with rats, a close-up of one white rat, silver gates, a devotee stepping carefully, a zoom out.

## #8: Kailasa Temple, Ellora (Maharashtra) (4:40–5:55)

**VO:**
"Stop for a second and imagine this. You have **no machines, no cranes, no dynamite.** Just chisels, hammers, and a mountain of solid basalt.
Now carve a multi-storey temple, with pillars, bridges, elephants and gods, **out of that single rock.**
And start at the **top.**
That's the Kailasa temple at Ellora, cut in the 8th century under the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I. Estimates say **around 2 lakh tonnes of rock** were removed.
One mistake and there's no undo button. You can't glue a pillar back onto a mountain.
Where did all that rubble go? How did they plan it with no blueprint software? Historians have theories about teams, grids and generations of work, but **no complete record survives.**
Some say it was built in 18 years. Some say that's impossible. What do you think? **Tell me in the comments.**"

**Visuals:** drone descent into the carved pit, a time-lapse animation of the rock being "removed" top-down, life-size elephants, a tiny human figure for scale.

## #7: Ramappa Temple, Telangana (5:55–6:55)

**VO:**
"In Telangana stands a temple built in **1213 CE** by the Kakatiya dynasty, and it's named after its **sculptor, Ramappa**, not a king or a god.
It has stood for 800 years, through wars and earthquakes.
Its secret is said to be in the **bricks of its tower.** They are so light and porous that, according to local accounts, **they float on water.**
Its sandbox foundation, a bed of sand under the stone, is thought to act like a shock absorber against earthquakes.
In 2021 UNESCO made it a **World Heritage Site.** 800-year-old engineering that modern architects still study.
Floating bricks are strange. But the next temple has a flag that **refuses to obey the wind.**"

**Visuals:** a brick dropped into a bucket of water and bobbing up, dancer sculptures, a UNESCO plaque, a cross-section graphic of the sand foundation.

## #6: Jagannath Temple, Puri (Odisha) (6:55–8:10)

**VO:**
"Puri's Jagannath temple has more legends than almost any temple in India.
Devotees say the flag on top **flies against the direction of the wind.** That **no bird flies over** the main dome. That the Sudarshan Chakra on top **looks the same from every direction.**
And every single day, a priest climbs the 65-metre temple, **without a harness**, to change that flag. The tradition is centuries old, and it's said that if it's missed even once, the temple must close for 18 years.
Is the flag really defying the wind? Scientists suggest the tall temple creates **swirling air currents** behind it, so the flag can move against the breeze you feel on the ground.
Science explains the flag. **It doesn't explain the courage of the man who climbs up there every evening.**"

**Visuals:** a flag against a sunset, a priest scaling the spire, a wind-flow animation showing eddies behind the tower, the Rath Yatra crowd.

## #5: Konark Sun Temple (Odisha) (8:20–9:25)

**VO:**
"Konark was built in the 13th century by King Narasimhadeva I. It isn't just a temple, it's a giant **stone chariot of the Sun God**, with 24 carved wheels pulled by seven horses.
Those wheels are **working sundials.** Read the shadow on the spokes and you can tell the time within minutes.
And the legend? The main tower once held a **massive magnet**, so powerful that it pulled passing ships toward the shore and threw off their compasses. Sailors called it the *Black Pagoda.*
Historians say the magnet story is unproven, and the main tower collapsed centuries ago. But the sundial wheels **still work today.**"

**Visuals:** a sun moving across a stone wheel and the shadow sweeping the spokes, old ships at sea, a spinning compass needle, the ruined tower.

## #4: Brihadeeswarar Temple, Thanjavur (Tamil Nadu) (9:25–10:30)

**VO:**
"In 1010 CE, Raja Raja Chola I finished a temple so tall that it dominated the skyline for centuries. Its tower rises about **66 metres**, built largely of **granite**, in a region with **no granite quarry nearby.**
At the top sits a capstone commonly said to weigh **around 80 tonnes.** How did they lift it? One popular theory is a **ramp several kilometres long.**
You may have heard that this temple's **shadow never falls on the ground** at noon. That's a **myth.** It does cast a shadow. But the fact that people *want* to believe it tells you how impossible this building feels.
A thousand years later it's still a living temple, and part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site."

**Visuals:** a dawn tower, an animated ramp with elephants hauling stone, a "MYTH ❌" stamp over the shadow claim, a real shadow on the courtyard.

## #3: Kamakhya Temple, Guwahati (Assam) (10:30–11:25)

**VO:**
"On Nilachal hill in Assam stands **Kamakhya**, one of the most powerful Shakti Peethas.
There's **no idol** in the inner sanctum, only a natural rock and an underground spring that keeps it moist.
Every June, the temple **shuts its doors for three days** for the Ambubachi Mela. It's believed the goddess is in her annual cycle. When it reopens, lakhs of devotees and sadhus gather.
Some say the spring water **turns red** during this time. Scientists point to iron-rich minerals and vermilion. Devotees say it's the goddess herself.
In a country where this subject is often hidden, **here it's worshipped.** That may be Kamakhya's biggest mystery of all."

**Visuals:** the beehive dome of the temple, closed doors with monsoon rain, crowds of sadhus, a red cloth, a respectful wide shot. *(Keep the visuals respectful: no graphic imagery.)*

## #2: Tanot Mata Temple, Jaisalmer (Rajasthan) (11:25–12:25)

**VO:**
"Remember the bombs that didn't explode?
**1965. India–Pakistan war.** The Pakistani army shelled the area around the Tanot Mata temple, near the border in the Thar desert. Thousands of shells, it's said.
But the temple was **untouched.** Many shells that fell nearby **never exploded.**
In 1971 it happened again. After the war the **Border Security Force** took over the temple's care, and **BSF jawans still perform its aarti today.**
Some of those unexploded shells are **on display inside the temple.**
Was it faulty ammunition? Soft sand? Or something else? Soldiers who served here have their own answer."

**Visuals:** desert night, animated shells arcing over dunes, BSF jawans in uniform performing aarti, a museum shelf with rusted shells, a victory pillar.

## #1: Kedarnath Temple (Uttarakhand) (12:25–13:40)

**VO:**
"June 2013. A cloudburst and a glacial-lake burst sent a **wall of water, mud and boulders** crashing down on Kedarnath.
Thousands of lives were lost. Entire towns and buildings were **wiped away.**
But the 1,000-year-old stone temple **still stood.**
Because right behind it, a **huge boulder rolled down and stopped**, and split the flood around the temple like a shield.
Today devotees call it **Bhim Shila** and worship it.
Engineers point to the temple's massive interlocking stone and its location. But ask anyone who was there and they'll tell you: **that rock stopped exactly where it had to.**
Science tells us *how* the temple survived. **Faith asks *why* that rock stopped there.**"

**Visuals:** stormy Himalayas, flood footage-style reconstruction (not real disaster footage of victims), the boulder behind the temple, today's pilgrims touching Bhim Shila, a slow pull-out to the snowy peaks.
